\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We organize related work by the problem solved and the information consumed.
Every per-system boundary below is re-verified against the original text
before submission. \emph{[citations to be verified throughout this section]}

\paragraph{Iteration-level serving and paged memory.}
Orca and PagedAttention/vLLM established iteration-granularity scheduling and
physical KV paging inside a request's lifetime; they do not manage the
session-level lifecycle of state across updates.

\paragraph{Session-level KV tiering and restoration.}
CachedAttention, Pensieve, HCache, Strata, Bidaw, Mooncake, LMCache, and
engine-built swapping place paused-session state across tiers and reload it on
return. Their restoration triggers are queue depth or request arrival; none
consumes the release times a clocked workload commits.

\paragraph{Hybrid restoration and recomputation.}
InferCept, Cake, and CacheFlow shorten the restoration segment by mixing
reload with recomputation; the segment stays on the critical path.

\paragraph{Next-use awareness and prefetching.}
SYMPHONY, InfiniGen, ECHO, KVFlow, and learned prefix caching show that lead
time has value, but their signals---application hints, in-layer prediction,
workflow step distance---differ in source and action domain from a periodic
rhythm.

\paragraph{Duplex and real-time speech serving.}
The nearest family. Metronome frames per-update deadlines as periodic
real-time tasks and constrains retained state by windowing, holding the
windowed state fully resident; LiveServe evicts on observed next-use estimates
and prefetches on speech onset---estimates, not workload-committed times;
VoxServe and stage-graph substrates schedule request-scoped soft deadlines.

\paragraph{Service objectives derived from request information.}
JITServe, DistServe, Sarathi-Serve, and Clockwork derive scheduling targets
from application requirements for request-level service, not for cross-period
session state.

\paragraph{Periodic tasks and deadline scheduling.}
Classical periodic-task theory and deadline schedulers supply the vocabulary
of offsets and schedulability; they do not manage state movement or
restoration timing.

\paragraph{Windows, compression, and alternative state structures.}
These change the semantics and size of retained state---a dimension orthogonal
to residency management. We treat them as composable workload parameters in a
quality--resource trade-off, not as baselines to defeat.

\paragraph{Tiered memory, placement, and heterogeneous execution.}
DirectKV, FlashGen, NEO, OrbitFlow, and FlexGen change the hardware conditions
that set restoration costs; they shape how our conclusions extrapolate rather
than serving as direct comparisons.

Across the nine families, and pending per-paper verification: no known work
uses the next-use times committed by a periodic workload to drive residency
reclamation and state restoration, moving state preparation off the critical
path without changing the application's retention semantics. The scope in
which this positioning holds is the scope in which our assumptions hold, as
delimited in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.
