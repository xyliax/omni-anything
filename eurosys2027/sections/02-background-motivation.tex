\subsection{Periodic Work and Playback Pacing}
\label{sec:request-classes}
\label{sec:formulation}

An interaction session repeatedly processes new input while retaining history across updates.
For a session with period $T$, each update is released at a tick and has a soft deadline at the next tick.
Update latency is measured from its scheduled tick to completion, including any delay before submission.
Once an update stops accessing its historical KV, that state can remain idle until the next update, even while the output is still playing.
This interval creates an opportunity to reclaim GPU space while preserving the session's history.

Speech illustrates why computing an update can take less time than playing its output.
In models trained for time-aligned interaction, generation follows playback progress: each update typically produces content for the corresponding playback interval.
For example, MiniCPM-o 4.5 trains on text and speech assigned to temporal chunks and uses Time-Aligned Interleaving to adjust generation to accumulated playback progress~\cite{cui2026minicpmo45}.
A long response therefore unfolds across updates, with new input incorporated along the way.
Playback determines the useful pace of generation, while the hardware can compute that content much faster.

For example, a one-second update in MiniCPM-o 4.5 processes ten encoded audio positions and generates about three to four text tokens at normal speaking speed, excluding prompt and control positions~\cite{cui2026minicpmo45}.
Its public llama.cpp-omni implementation reports an audio prefill stage of about 21\,ms and a backbone decoding cost of about 38\,ms/token on an RTX 4090 in FP16~\cite{llamacppomni2026readme}.
Combining these figures gives an estimated 114--152\,ms of backbone decoding per second of speech, or 135--173\,ms including the reported prefill stage.
The reported stage timings omit speech synthesis and runtime overhead, and leave batch size and retained context length unspecified.
Complete update time must therefore be measured at the target concurrency and history length.

\subsection{Why KV Capacity Can Bind First}
\label{sec:capacity-problem}
\label{sec:slack-conditions}

Playback pacing keeps typical generation per update small, while the retained history accumulates over successive updates.
A session can therefore accumulate a large KV cache even when each update adds only a small amount of work and state.
Keeping that cache on the GPU throughout the period consumes capacity even while the session is idle.
Training does not impose a strict per-update token cap, and attention becomes more expensive as history grows.
The available compute slack therefore depends on both the workload and its execution cost.

The relevant comparison is between the memory and computation required by all admitted sessions.
Consider $N$ sessions with a common period $T$, a maximum retained length of $L$ positions per session during an update, and $\kappa$ KV bytes per position, with no KV sharing.
Let $G_{\mathrm{KV}}$ be the GPU capacity available for KV after other allocations.
Let $C_N(L)$ be the total execution time required for one update from every session under the chosen grouping and batching policy.
It includes input processing, generation, and runtime overhead, counting overlapping execution once and excluding idle gaps between scheduled releases.
Ignoring allocation rounding, the full-residency capacity limit leaves execution slack when
\begin{equation}
  L_{\mathrm{mem}}=\frac{G_{\mathrm{KV}}}{N\kappa},
  \qquad C_N(L_{\mathrm{mem}})<T.
  \label{eq:memory-before-compute}
\end{equation}
The remaining aggregate execution budget is $T-C_N(L_{\mathrm{mem}})$.
Memory multiplexing must fit its additional execution and interference costs within this budget while also satisfying individual deadlines, restoration windows, and allocation constraints.
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} tests whether additional periodic work can actually complete when retained state exceeds a fixed GPU KV budget.
Its joint completion and allocation observations support a capacity benefit for the measured workload, without interpreting per-request latency as aggregate GPU busy time.
The analytical estimates below separately examine how model geometry and device resources affect this opportunity.

The speech decoder's separate 4,096-position limit is also included in the memory reservation~\cite{openbmb2026minicpmconfig}.
The combined KV reservation is $K_{\mathrm{sess}}\approx5.86$\,GiB per session; we assume another $R=20$\,GiB for weights and other allocations.
Let $M$ be the GPU memory budget, approximating nominal capacities as GiB.
With $G_{\mathrm{KV}}=M-R$, the memory-only session limit is $N_{\mathrm{mem}}=\lfloor G_{\mathrm{KV}}/K_{\mathrm{sess}}\rfloor$.
Each row assumes that all $N_{\mathrm{mem}}$ sessions form one batch near the context limit, with enough room in each history for the update's new positions.
This is the largest batch that fits within the KV memory budget.
Device capacities and bandwidths follow vendor specifications~\cite{nvidia2021a100,nvidia2023h100,nvidia2026rtxpro,nvidia2026h200,amd2025mi300x}.

We estimate backbone prefill and decode by separating linear layers from attention and applying a roofline model to each component~\cite{nvidia2023performance}.
For component $j$, with byte traffic $D_j$ and floating-point operation count $O_j$, the time estimate is
\[
  \widehat t_j=\max\left(\frac{D_j}{\eta_B B_{\mathrm{mem}}},\frac{O_j}{\eta_F F}\right),
\]
where $B_{\mathrm{mem}}$ is rated GPU memory bandwidth and $F$ is a dense BF16 matrix-throughput reference.
The central estimates in both tables assume bandwidth efficiency $\eta_B=0.8$ and compute efficiency $\eta_F=0.25$, without empirical calibration.
Weights are read once for the whole batch, while the attention traffic includes each session's own KV.
For MiniCPM-o, each modeled update consists of a ten-position incremental prefill followed by four sequential decode forward passes.
The component times are summed over these stages; parameter counts and traffic accounting appear in the supplementary material.
The RTX estimate is higher than those for A100 and H100 because it combines lower memory bandwidth with a larger batch and therefore more total KV traffic.

Under these assumptions, estimated backbone execution time, including memory-access costs, is below one second in all five configurations.
Table~\ref{tab:hardware-capacity-projection} also reports sensitivity to the assumed efficiencies for each GPU.
KV reuse also affects the estimate: doubling the modeled attention traffic raises the RTX estimate to about 643\,ms at the assumed efficiencies, or 1.01\,s if bandwidth efficiency also falls to 50\%.
Staggering sessions can reduce batch sizes and increase weight traffic and total execution time, consuming part of the estimated margin.
Establishing the complete-workload condition in Equation~\ref{eq:memory-before-compute} requires measurements of usable memory, actual batching and traffic, input encoding, speech generation, and runtime overhead.

The supplementary material details auxiliary KV reservations and other memory budgets.

For Moshi at its memory ceiling, the RTX estimate is about 74.2\,ms, leaving only 5.8\,ms of its 80\,ms period for omitted stages.
With compute efficiency fixed, reducing bandwidth efficiency to 70\% or 50\% raises that backbone estimate to 104.7\% or 146.6\% of the period.
The compute slack available for memory multiplexing therefore depends on the workload and hardware together.

\subsection{Bandwidth Limits on Memory Reuse}
\label{sec:opportunity}

Periodic eviction and restoration allow idle KV to spend part of each period in host memory.
Consider a steady-state schedule with a common period $T$, fixed history sizes, and no shared KV.
Let $e_i$ be the bytes evicted from session $i$ and restored once per period, and let $B_{\mathrm{eff}}$ be the effective H2D bandwidth available for restoration.
The total restoration volume must satisfy
\begin{equation}
  E=\sum_i e_i \le B_{\mathrm{eff}}T.
  \label{eq:transfer-budget}
\end{equation}
The fraction of the transfer budget used is $E/(B_{\mathrm{eff}}T)$; continuous transfers reach the ceiling $B_{\mathrm{eff}}T$.
The effective bandwidth must account for competing traffic, including incremental host backing.
Each copy must fit within the interval between safe eviction and the next tick, with its GPU destination allocated before copying begins.

\paragraph{Why full eviction can fail.}
Evicting every session's entire history makes transfer demand grow with total retained state.
Once this demand exceeds the budget in Equation~\ref{eq:transfer-budget}, continuous transfers cannot restore every session's history within each period.
A single large restoration can also exceed its available window or require more destination space than is free.
For illustration, assume eight histories of 4.5\,GiB, a one-second period, and 24\,GiB/s of effective H2D bandwidth.
Under these assumptions, full restoration requires 36\,GiB per period, or 1.5 seconds of copying; evicting 2\,GiB per session reduces this to 16\,GiB and about 0.67 seconds.
The partial-eviction plan must also fit the available copy windows and GPU space.
Partial eviction allows the server to retain the state that cannot be restored in time.

\paragraph{From transferred bytes to usable capacity.}
Evicted KV saves GPU space from the moment it is freed until its restoration destination is allocated.
For session $i$, let $w_i$ be the duration of this interval averaged over its $e_i$ evicted bytes, so $0\le w_i\le T$.
Let $\Delta G_{\mathrm{peak}}$ be the reduction in peak allocation relative to full residency and $\overline{\Delta G}$ the average reduction over the period.
Then
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \Delta G_{\mathrm{peak}}
    &\le \overline{\Delta G}
    =\frac{1}{T}\sum_i e_i w_i \\
    &\le E\le B_{\mathrm{eff}}T.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:memory-gain}
\end{equation}
Thus, $B_{\mathrm{eff}}T$ bounds the reduction in peak GPU KV allocation through memory multiplexing.
The capacity gain depends on the duration and overlap of these intervals.
Earlier destination allocation shortens these intervals.
Staggering sessions lets one session's eviction free space for another's restoration, provided that overlapping allocations fit in GPU memory and execution fits within the available time.
