\subsection{Periodic Work and Playback Pacing}
\label{sec:request-classes}
\label{sec:formulation}

An interaction session repeatedly processes new input while carrying history across updates.
With period $T$, each update is released at a tick and has a soft deadline at the next tick.
Service latency includes both waiting and execution; delaying submission does not move the deadline.
Once an update stops accessing its historical KV, that state can remain idle until the next update even while its output is being played.
This interval creates an opportunity to reclaim GPU space while preserving the session's history.

Speech illustrates why an update can finish well before its output has been consumed.
In models trained for time-aligned interaction, generation follows playback progress: each update typically produces content for the corresponding playback interval.
For example, MiniCPM-o 4.5 trains on text and speech assigned to temporal chunks and uses Time-Aligned Interleaving to adjust generation to accumulated playback progress~\cite{cui2026minicpmo45}.
A long response therefore unfolds across updates, with new input incorporated along the way.
Playback determines the useful pace of generation, while the hardware can compute that content much faster.

For a concrete scale, MiniCPM-o 4.5 uses one-second updates, with ten incoming audio positions and about three to four generated text tokens at normal speaking speed~\cite{cui2026minicpmo45}.
These counts exclude prompt and control positions.
Its public llama.cpp-omni implementation reports an audio prefill stage of about 21\,ms and a backbone decoding cost of about 38\,ms/token on an RTX 4090 in F16~\cite{llamacppomni2026readme}.
Combining these external figures gives 114--152\,ms of backbone decoding per second of speech, or about 6.6--8.8 times the playback rate, and 135--173\,ms including the reported prefill stage.
This is a scale estimate, not a complete cycle measurement: speech synthesis and runtime overhead also require time, and the reported stage timings do not specify batch size or retained context length.
Those costs must be measured at the intended concurrency and history length.

\subsection{Why KV Capacity Can Bind First}
\label{sec:capacity-problem}
\label{sec:slack-conditions}

Playback pacing keeps typical generation per update small, while the retained history accumulates over successive updates.
The session can therefore add only a small amount of work and state each period while eventually retaining a large KV cache.
Keeping that cache on the GPU throughout the cycle consumes capacity even while the session is idle.
Training does not impose a strict token cap, and attention becomes more expensive as history grows; the resulting margin therefore depends on both the workload and its execution cost.

The relevant comparison is between the memory and computation required by all admitted sessions.
Consider $N$ sessions with a common period, $L$ retained positions each, and $\kappa$ KV bytes per position, with no KV sharing.
Let $G_{\mathrm{KV}}$ be the GPU capacity available for KV after other allocations, and let $C_N(L)$ be the time required for the complete set of periodic work under the chosen execution policy.
It includes input processing, generation, and runtime overhead, with batching accounted for.
Ignoring allocation rounding, memory binds first when
\begin{equation}
  L_{\mathrm{mem}}=\frac{G_{\mathrm{KV}}}{N\kappa},
  \qquad C_N(L_{\mathrm{mem}})<T.
  \label{eq:memory-before-compute}
\end{equation}
The remaining budget is $T-C_N(L_{\mathrm{mem}})$.
Equation~\ref{eq:memory-before-compute} states the operating condition that admission must establish: the complete workload still has time available when full residency fills the KV pool.
This is the margin that can absorb the computation and interference costs of memory multiplexing.
The resulting schedule must also satisfy each session's deadline.

Table~\ref{tab:hardware-capacity-projection} fixes the public backbone context limit at 40,960 positions and solves for the number of resident sessions, rather than fixing concurrency and varying context.
The speech decoder's separate 4,096-position limit is also included in the memory reservation~\cite{openbmb2026minicpmconfig}.
The combined KV reservation is $S\approx5.86$\,GiB per session, with another $R=20$\,GiB assumed for weights and other allocations.
Approximating nominal GPU capacities as GiB budgets, the memory-only ceiling is $N_{\mathrm{mem}}=\lfloor(M-R)/S\rfloor$.
Each row then batches all $N_{\mathrm{mem}}$ sessions for an update near the context limit; the initial history leaves space for the new positions so the limit is not exceeded.
This is a capacity-saturating batch assumption, not a claim that the GPU achieves peak arithmetic throughput or that this batch is feasible under every phase arrangement.
Device capacities and bandwidths follow vendor specifications~\cite{nvidia2021a100,nvidia2023h100,nvidia2026rtxpro,nvidia2026h200,amd2025mi300x}.

We estimate backbone prefill and decode by separating linear layers from attention and applying a roofline model to each component~\cite{nvidia2023performance}.
For component $j$, with byte traffic $D_j$ and operation count $O_j$, the time estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat t_j=\max\left(\frac{D_j}{\eta_B B},\frac{O_j}{\eta_F F}\right),
  \label{eq:batched-cost-estimate}
\end{equation}
where $B$ is rated local-memory bandwidth and $F$ is a dense BF16 matrix-throughput reference.
The table uses $\eta_B=0.5$ and $\eta_F=0.25$ as explicit scenario assumptions.
The backbone's projection and MLP matrices contain about 6.95 billion parameters and its output head another 0.62 billion, giving about 15.1\,GB of BF16 matrix weights per forward pass~\cite{openbmb2026minicpmconfig}.
Weights are read once for the whole batch, while the attention traffic includes each session's own KV.
The prefill estimate processes ten positions together, reuses their historical KV, and computes logits only for the last position; four sequential decode forwards follow, including a final state update when needed.
Compute time is the sum of prefill and decode; cycle used is this time divided by the one-second period.
The RTX matrix reference is approximately 240\,TFLOP/s, inferred from its FP32 rating and the GB202 dense BF16/FP32-accumulation ratio, rather than its advertised low-precision AI rate~\cite{nvidia2026rtxpro,nvidia2025blackwell}.
The RTX row takes longer than A100 and H100 in this model because it combines lower memory bandwidth with a larger batch and more private KV traffic.
These rows compare each GPU at its own memory ceiling, not at a common batch size.

This conditional model places backbone work below the one-second period on all five configurations, but the remainder is not measured GPU idle time or a full-pipeline deadline guarantee.
Using 40--70\% bandwidth and 15--40\% matrix efficiency instead yields approximately 266--476, 161--282, 397--710, 205--366, and 260--463\,ms in table order; these are assumption ranges, not confidence intervals.
KV reuse is also material: doubling the modeled attention traffic raises the RTX estimate to about 1.01\,s.
Usable memory, actual batching and traffic, input encoding, speech generation, and runtime overhead require measurement before these estimates can establish the complete-workload condition in Equation~\ref{eq:memory-before-compute}.


\subsection{Bandwidth Limits on Memory Reuse}
\label{sec:opportunity}

Offloading replaces continuous GPU residency with repeated restoration.
If session $i$ evicts $e_i$ bytes per cycle and $B_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the effective H2D bandwidth available to restoration, then the total restored state obeys
\begin{equation}
  E=\sum_i e_i \le B_{\mathrm{eff}}T.
  \label{eq:transfer-budget}
\end{equation}
The utilization of this transfer budget is $E/(B_{\mathrm{eff}}T)$; continuous use reaches the ceiling $B_{\mathrm{eff}}T$.
The bandwidth must account for competing traffic, including incremental host backups.
Each copy must also fit between safe eviction and the next tick and obtain its GPU destination before it starts.

\paragraph{Why full eviction can fail.}
Evicting every session's entire history makes transfer demand grow with total retained state.
Once that demand exceeds Equation~\ref{eq:transfer-budget}, all histories cannot be restored every period even with continuous transfers.
A single large restoration can also exceed its available window or require more destination space than is free.
For illustration, assume eight histories of 4.5\,GiB, a one-second period, and 24\,GiB/s of effective H2D bandwidth.
Full restoration needs 36\,GiB per cycle, or 1.5 seconds of copying; evicting 2\,GiB per session reduces this to 16\,GiB and about 0.67 seconds.
These are assumed resource values, not measured capacity results; the partial plan still needs feasible copy windows and GPU allocations.
Partial eviction allows the server to retain the state that cannot be restored in time.

\paragraph{From transferred bytes to usable capacity.}
A byte saves GPU space only from its eviction until its restoration destination is allocated.
For a steady cycle with fixed history sizes and no shared KV, let $w_i$ be the average time that session $i$'s $e_i$ evicted bytes spend without a GPU allocation.
Let $\Delta G_{\mathrm{peak}}$ be the reduction in peak allocation relative to full residency and $\overline{\Delta G}$ the average reduction over the cycle.
Then
\begin{equation}
  \Delta G_{\mathrm{peak}}
  \le \overline{\Delta G}
  =\frac{1}{T}\sum_i e_i w_i
  \le B_{\mathrm{eff}}T.
  \label{eq:memory-gain}
\end{equation}
Thus, $B_{\mathrm{eff}}T$ bounds the state that can be reused each cycle.
The capacity gain depends on the duration and overlap of the eviction intervals.
Earlier destination allocation shortens these intervals.
Staggering sessions helps one session's eviction supply space for another's restoration, but both their memory overlap and execution cost must fit the schedule.
