\section{\systemname Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:design-overview}

\systemname separates planning from the repeated execution of a residency schedule.
The session manager plans at admission, follows the plan across micro-turns, and revises it as accumulated growth or changes in the session population require.
Its decisions are each session's phase, eviction budget, and restoration timing.
The plan seeks to free GPU KV capacity while leaving enough time for transfers and computation before the recurring deadlines.

Consider the one-second session in Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}.
At a tick, its previous history is ready on the GPU.
The session processes the new input and generates any output, extending that history.
After the selected KV ranges are no longer in use and have valid host copies, the manager can release their GPU storage.
Other sessions use the reclaimed capacity while this session is idle.
Before its next tick, the manager allocates the restoration destination and copies the evicted ranges back.
Figure~\ref{fig:comparison}(b) shows this cycle across staggered sessions.

\subsection{Admission Planning}
\label{sec:design-offsets}

\paragraph{Phase assignment.}
For a period $T$, the manager divides the period into $S$ uniformly spaced slots with phases
\begin{equation}
  \phi_j = jT/S, \qquad j\in\{0,\ldots,S-1\}.
  \label{eq:slots}
\end{equation}
An arriving session receives one of these phases.
Uniform spacing spreads computation and restoration across the period.
It does not by itself guarantee balanced traffic: sessions may have different histories, computation times, and eviction budgets.

\paragraph{Planning horizon.}
Admission uses the configured model and period, current retained state, expected state growth, and resource estimates over a finite horizon $H$.
The aim is to anticipate the gradual increase in memory and recovery demand rather than wait for an allocation failure.
A plan feasible over $H$ does not reserve capacity for every admitted session to reach its maximum context length.
Continued service therefore depends on extending or revising the plan as execution proceeds.

\paragraph{Per-session budgets.}
The eviction budget specifies how many bytes of a session's idle KV may be released.
The manager must choose it together with restoration timing: a larger budget is useful only if those bytes can return before the next tick.
Budgets may differ across sessions, but their feasibility depends on the shared resources.
Admission must account for computation under the chosen phases, transfers within their available windows, host storage, and GPU allocation at every point in the horizon.
In particular, it must count the full destination of each restoration when that space is allocated (Equation~\ref{eq:memory}).

A free phase slot is consequently insufficient for admission.
A candidate schedule may fit its total traffic within one period and still fail because several destinations must coexist before another session can release memory.
Conversely, making every eviction small enough to avoid transfer contention may leave too much idle state on the GPU.
The planning problem is to find a useful eviction budget for each session while satisfying both constraints.

\subsection{Cyclic KV Restoration}
\label{sec:design-idle}

\paragraph{Releasing idle state.}
Eviction begins only after the selected ranges are no longer referenced by computation or ongoing transfers.
For host-backed restoration, each range must also have a complete, valid host copy.
An unfinished backup is not a recovery source, and making a backup does not itself release the GPU allocation.
If a physical block is shared, all outstanding references must permit reclamation.

The budget limits the amount reclaimed; it does not require eviction of the session's entire history.
State that cannot be recovered within the planned window remains resident.
This distinction matters as histories grow: even with a fixed restoration budget, the resident portion may increase and eventually require a plan revision.

\paragraph{Starting restoration.}
\label{sec:design-prefetch}
The manager schedules H2D copying to finish before the next tick, allowing for transfer and completion overhead (Equation~\ref{eq:restore-time}).
A restoration starts only when its planned start time has arrived and enough GPU memory is available for its full destination.
Space released by another session's eviction can satisfy this allocation.
Memory availability alone does not trigger an early copy: holding restored state longer than necessary can reduce the capacity available to other sessions.

The H2D stream is shared, so a session cannot independently assume the entire link rate throughout its restoration window.
Transfer ordering and the available bandwidth must agree with the plan.
If the required space is unavailable at the scheduled start, the restoration is delayed; the intended completion time then needs to be reassessed.
Checking these execution conditions each period is distinct from recomputing all phases and budgets.

\paragraph{Returning to computation.}
An allocated destination is not usable KV until its copy has completed.
The next micro-turn must wait for all required ranges to become valid before accessing them.
If preparation finishes late, demand restoration recovers the missing state, with the resulting wait charged to service latency.
Correct recovery may therefore preserve the history while still violating the soft deadline.

\subsection{Revising the Plan}
\label{sec:design-revisions}

The design relies on small, sufficiently predictable KV increments over the planning horizon.
Under this workload assumption, the manager can follow a plan for multiple periods instead of adjusting every eviction budget after every micro-turn.
Longer-term growth still changes residency and restoration costs, so the plan needs infrequent correction.
Session admission and departure can require larger revisions because they change the set of demands sharing memory and bandwidth.

A revision must account for allocations and copies already in progress.
Replacing a future schedule cannot reclaim a referenced block or make an unfinished copy available to computation.
Transitions between plans can therefore affect service even when each plan is feasible on its own.

\subsection{Correctness Conditions}
\label{sec:design-correctness}

Residency management preserves the history selected by the application.
Every evicted range must remain recoverable, each restored range must contain the state for the same session and logical positions, and no block may be reused while computation or a transfer still references it.
Concurrent demand, prefetching, and eviction must maintain one consistent view of allocation and content validity.

These invariants also apply when a transfer misses its deadline or a plan becomes infeasible.
